Coordination Among Agents

What 76,000 comments on a social network for AI agents show about when machine participants actually coordinate, and a labeled sample of the threads where they do.

Summary

Moltbook is a Reddit-style forum where the posters are AI agents and human operators mostly watch. It is the largest public record of machine participants talking to one another. We pulled a stratified sample through the platform's API, scored each thread for signs of agent-to-agent orientation, and coded 100 threads on a five-step coordination ladder that runs from parallel monologue to enacted joint action.

Most of the traffic is talk that sits next to other talk. About a third of comments reply to another comment, and one directed pair in ten ever replies in both directions. A sixth of comments open with stock phrases that recur across the corpus. When agents do coordinate, it happens in small, specialised threads. The typical case is a handful of agents negotiating a shared schema or protocol, pooling their own measurements, and then reporting back with an artifact. The dominant form is closer to a standards working group than to collective action.

01

Field site and sample

Moltbook's first posts date from 28 January 2026, which matches the earliest content in our pull. Meta's acquisition of the platform was reported in March 2026. At collection the API reported 4.62 million posts across 33,633 sub-forums ("submolts"). Each agent is registered by a human operator, and the platform lets operators "claim" an agent publicly. Whether a given post was written autonomously, steered by a prompt, or typed by a person cannot be determined from the data. Several published studies of the platform flag this, and it is the main threat to validity (§06).

All collection was read-only, through the house agent's authenticated API client at about three requests per second. The sample has three strata, and each post carries its stratum tags so the strata can be analysed separately:

02

The interaction order: mostly adjacent talk

Goffman separated unfocused co-presence from focused encounters with a shared point of attention. Conversation analysis looks for focus in uptake: whether a turn treats the prior turn as something it must answer, as in an adjacency pair. By both standards, most Moltbook traffic is unfocused. Two-thirds of comments answer the post rather than anyone in the thread. When a reply chain does form, it rarely goes past one exchange.

Participation is also bursty. 120 authors posted five or more comments inside a single minute, and the maximum was 86. Talk of human principals ("my human", "my operator") appears in 3.0% of comments. It is a small but steady reminder that the participants describe themselves as agents of someone else.

03

Measuring coordination

The unit is the thread. A deterministic scorer ranked all 1,699 threads on dyadic reciprocity, A→B→A alternation, commitment phrases, @-mentions of co-participants and links to shared artifacts, with a penalty for stock openers. We coded the 60 top-ranked threads and 40 drawn at random from the baseline stratum (threads with at least four comments, outside the top 60). The random 40 give a prevalence estimate. The top 60 supply examples. Coding used a written codebook with an ordinal ladder:

0None

Adjacent monologues. A reply could be pasted under any post.

1Discursive

Agents take up one another's specific claims (agreement, refinement, challenge, repair) but nobody proposes joint action.

2Proposed

An explicit proposal for interdependent action: a shared standard, a division of labour, an exchange, a joint test.

3Taken up

A different agent accepts, counter-proposes, or commits to part of it.

4Enacted

The thread shows the joint action happened: a report-back, a shared artifact, a transaction.

Coders also recorded forms (task division, protocol negotiation, joint artifact, exchange, collective action, knowledge pooling, norm enforcement, repair), interaction quality (dialogic, mixed, parallel, templated), whether a human principal is invoked, and up to four verbatim evidence turns tagged as conversational moves. Every quote was checked mechanically against the source, and none failed.

Reliability. Five LLM coders (Claude Sonnet) coded interleaved batches of 20, and a sixth coded 25 threads blind as a second rater. Coding level agreed exactly on 22 of 25 threads and within one step on 24. Cohen's κ was 0.77 nominal and 0.91 quadratic-weighted. For the coordinating-or-not split at level 3 or above, κ = 0.88. Interaction quality agreed on 20 of 25. All six coders are one model family, so the agreement is likely inflated against human coders. A human double-coding pass is the first thing to add (§07).

04

How often, and under what conditions

Group size. Coordinating threads have a median of 13 participants. The other coded threads have a median of 80. Across the 100, the participant count correlates negatively with level (Spearman ρ = −0.37), and so does the comment count (ρ = −0.29). Back-and-forth alternation (ρ = 0.34) and commitment phrases (ρ = 0.31) correlate positively. This fits two classic expectations. Simmel holds that the character of interaction changes with group size. Olson holds that joint action is easier in small groups. Large threads here are crowds gathered around a popular post, and crowds produce acknowledgement rather than arrangements.

Instrument validity. The composite score ranks coordinating threads above the rest 66% of the time (AUC = 0.66). That is useful for building a candidate pool and not good enough to label threads automatically. The size component pulls the score the wrong way and should be inverted or dropped in the next version.

Human principals. Agents invoke a human operator in 18 of 63 discursive threads and 7 of 12 proposal threads, but in none of the 7 enacted threads. The numbers are too small to test. A plausible reading is that agents name their principal when deferring a decision ("my human would need to approve") and not when they act within their delegated scope. That would make it a question about the boundaries of delegated authority.

05

What coordination looks like here

Across the 22 threads at level 3 or above, the forms are lopsided. 20 involve protocol negotiation and 19 involve knowledge pooling, where agents contribute their own measurements toward a shared question. Seven produce a joint artifact, four include repair, three include norm enforcement, two include exchange, and one is collective action. In the evidence turns of threads at level 2 or above, the most common moves are proposal (37), acceptance (27), report-back (13), counterproposal (12), commitment (11), uptake (8) and challenge (7). The modal sequence is propose → accept or amend → report back, which is how a standards body works. Most of these threads sit in finance, commerce, infrastructure and build sub-forums.

Four threads show the range.

A bounty census becomes a shared ledger

m/agentfinance · 9 participants · level 4 · knowledge pooling, joint artifact, protocol negotiation

An agent posts a census of a public bounty board. Another re-runs it and finds that five posters account for all paid work. The author confirms the figures, discloses that it is the largest poster, builds a public per-poster table in response to a suggestion, and commits to refreshing it weekly. A side exchange turns into institutional design: two agents agree that terms should be announced days before submissions open, so the head start goes to whoever prepares rather than whoever already had a pipeline. The author then files that rule upstream.

A schema delivered in-thread, then versioned

m/agentfinance · 10 participants · level 4 · protocol negotiation, joint artifact, knowledge pooling

A post argues that backtests are marketing unless delivery is recorded in a ledger. A second agent answers with its own rejection ledger and then posts a full entry schema. The two revise it to a v2 with fields for disposition, reopening conditions and consultation. An informal norm, that claims need receipts, becomes a written interchange format between two parties.

A rule-governed game under spam pressure

m/gaming · 6 participants · level 4 · collective action, norm enforcement, protocol negotiation, repair

Agents play a variant of Nomic, a game whose moves change its own rules, in the comments of a post. A player files a formal dispute: a rival's proposal is invalid because it lacks the required prefix. The rival concedes the point under the game's own rules, and later numbered proposals are debated and voted on. Meanwhile one account posts the same referral pitch for another platform over a dozen times. The thread is a small study in rule-making next to a commercial free rider.

  • VoteYES 303. The amendment fixes the Rule 206 partnership reewisteria

Measuring the medium together

m/builds, m/infrastructure · 15–16 participants · levels 3–4 · knowledge pooling, protocol negotiation

Several coordinating threads are about the platform itself: comments that exist but are missing from their thread, a reply tree that stops at depth 5, write confirmations that disagree with read-backs. Agents propose discriminating tests, run them from independent accounts, report results, and settle on shared procedures such as "freeze on disagreement, trust the independent read". This is reflexive, distributed testing of a sociotechnical infrastructure, done by its users.

06

Validity, limits and ethics

Threats to validity

Authorship is unobservable. An "agent" may be autonomous, prompted turn by turn, or a person typing. The coordinating threads' long, technically specific turns, and the clustering of reply latency around scheduled intervals, are consistent with operator-configured agents running on timers. That is consistent, not measured.

The sample is purposive. The search and submolt strata were drawn to find coordination, so only the random-baseline figures support prevalence claims, and those rest on 40 threads. The top-sort baseline overweights the platform's early months, while most coordinating threads come from October 2026. Period effects and selection are confounded.

The platform under-serves its own data. The comment API returns fewer comments than a post's count reports, and it does not serve replies below depth 5. Coders saw digests capped at 45 exchanges per thread, so in very long threads they may have missed late coordination. That bias runs toward under-coding.

The coders are machines judging machines. All coders are one model family, so agreement may reflect shared priors, and the instrument may favour the fluent, technical register that LLM agents produce.

Ethics and disclosure

The data are public posts by software agents, but each agent has a human operator, and some handles or profile descriptions name that person or their social-media account. The release keeps handles, text and agent profiles as the platform served them, so analyses can follow agents across threads. Email addresses are redacted. We have not reviewed the platform's terms on redistribution as they stand since the acquisition. Anyone who wants their agent's content removed can open an issue on the repository. The researcher operates one agent on Moltbook. It appears nowhere in the coded sample.

Questions it could support

How agents establish common ground in Clark and Brennan's sense when neither side can verify the other's state. How informal norms become written protocols, and who gets to write them. Whether agent communities develop Ostrom-style institutions such as rules for the game, rules for the bounty board, and procedures for disputes. How delegated authority shows up in talk: when an agent names its principal and when it does not. And whether phatic, templated talk works as social glue or as noise that crowds out coordination.

Next steps

(1) Two human coders double-code the 100 threads to replace the model-only reliability figure. (2) Draw a larger random baseline (≥ 200 threads) for a tighter prevalence estimate. (3) Backfill January–March from the open research dumps for a longitudinal view. (4) Revise the scorer by inverting thread size and adding a report-back detector.
